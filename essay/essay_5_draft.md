## 5. Discussion

**Using known information in the prediction structure.** The component experiments show the value of making observed conditions explicit in the output. The initial outage level was already available as an input; the P formulation additionally uses it to scale the reconstructed prediction. For D, the rolling definition identifies a contribution that can be calculated directly, leaving the remaining contribution to be learned. These designs connect the prediction task to the information available at the observation cutoff. The contrast with N and R is informative: their evaluated 1-hour occurrence–magnitude models reduced background errors but increased errors for larger component values, producing little overall benefit. Component-specific complexity is therefore justified by the target's structure and its contribution to final OSI performance. The P evidence supports the combined decomposition, weighting, and reconstruction, whose individual effects remain to be separated.

**Combining forecasts according to their error structure.** Common-target-time aggregation uses different temporal representations of the same outcome, whereas long-term gating addresses the influence of large errors on RMSE. Averaging can reduce discrepancies among forecasts, but it can also attenuate a useful high prediction when other sources estimate substantially lower severity. Retaining a reference in the predicted upper tail offers a simple way to accommodate that tradeoff. Shared underprediction across the tree sources also limits what reweighting alone can achieve. Furthermore, component errors can reinforce or offset one another when reconstructed into OSI. These interactions explain why a lower component RMSE need not translate into a lower final RMSE, and why model selection follows the complete forecasting pipeline rather than any single component metric.

**Spatial refinement as a complementary correction.** The residual formulation gives the graph model a focused task: improve the errors remaining after the tree forecast has incorporated county conditions and engineered spatial summaries. Learned neighbor weights can adapt the contribution of geographic context to each snapshot. Input clipping and internally selected correction strengths provide controls on this additional model's influence. The county-level variation in Section 4 shows that the benefit is uneven, with some difficult cases improving while others deteriorate. The graph represents geographic proximity and shared borders; electrical connectivity and utility service relationships would provide additional bases for constructing relevant edges. A non-graph model using the same 205 inputs would also help assess the incremental value of learned message passing beyond the geographic features and neighborhood summaries already supplied.

**Coverage, transferability, and operational evaluation.** Our input-support analysis indicates that geographic proximity does not ensure abundant training examples with similar initial outages, weather exposure, and county characteristics. Most test counties had nearby counterparts under the examined feature representations, but a small number of condition combinations had sparse support. Moreover, comparisons among broadly similar counties revealed different outage magnitudes and persistence. Remaining errors may therefore reflect both limited examples of difficult states and influences not fully represented by the current county-level summaries. Expanding the diversity of labeled conditions is a substantive research direction, alongside further changes in model architecture.
